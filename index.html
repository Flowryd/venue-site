Wholesale markets · tokenisation

A market is a simple equation.

Tokenisation will not change what makes a market. It will only change where the market is made — and whether decentralised finance can deliver on its promise. That is being decided now.

Liz Towler · Founder & CEO, Flowryd

Set aside the complexity. Buyers and sellers make a trade. Trust and liquidity bring them back. A place to meet turns them into a market. That is it — it is the market. Everything else — the asset, the token, the technology, the currency — is detail. Important to get right, but it can't exist in isolation. To coin a real estate phrase, it's market, market, market.

This is worth remembering right now, because the entire tokenisation debate has fixed on the details. The token. The stablecoin. The ledger. The settlement money. The dollar's dominance, the safety of central bank money, the elegance of atomic settlement: all real, all necessary — and none of them, on their own, a market.

A market is made by the venue: the place where trust and liquidity meet. The UK Government's own Wholesale Financial Markets Digital Strategy has it right from the opening line — wholesale markets, it says, rely on a complex ecosystem, "including the venues through which assets are issued and traded." The venue is named first, and rightly so. From there, like most of us writing on tokenisation, it turns to the asset and the technology — understandably, those are the concrete parts. This piece simply picks the venue back up.

The asset is what is traded. The technology is how it moves. The venue is the market. And the currency it is priced in — the thing the current debate treats as decisive — is the most visible part, and the least.

The Champagne fairs already solved it

London has been the world's largest foreign exchange centre for the better part of two centuries, and at no point was that because of sterling. It has won the same argument, the same way, over and over.

The medieval Champagne fairs, then Bruges and Antwerp, established the model: a recurring venue with a fixed settlement period, where exposures were netted at the close. In 1571, Gresham copied Antwerp's bourse onto Cornhill as the Royal Exchange — a place to meet, with rules. The market followed the venue, not the merchandise. In the nineteenth century, the world cleared its trade credit through "bills on London" — not because Britain dominated trade, but because a bill accepted in London could be trusted and enforced.

Then the episode that ought to end the argument. In the late 1950s, a sterling crisis led the UK to restrict non-resident lending in its own currency. Rather than lose its role financing world trade, London switched to dollars — intermediating offshore dollar deposits under English law, outside American regulation. The eurodollar market was born, and modern London with it. The asset was American. The venue was British. London won the century's greatest currency market with someone else's currency. A century on, an entire market grew the same way — not from a statute or a standard policy, but from a room. Edward Lloyd's coffee house drew the shipowners, merchants and underwriters who came for the shipping news, and the market for insuring the voyages formed around the table. The venue made the market; the policy came after.

The lesson is eight hundred years old and has never once been contradicted: win the venue, and the asset comes to you.

The cornerstones the venue is built on

What makes London the venue is not mysterious, and it is not sterling. There are four things, and every one of them is a property of the place, not the unit.

Continuity. English commercial law runs unbroken for centuries; a contract enforceable today is enforceable the same way tomorrow. That predictability is why the world documents its wholesale trades under English law — and, crucially, it is a continuity through reinvention, not despite it. The bourse, the bill, the eurodollar, the screen: London has absorbed every wave of innovation and changed for the better without breaking the trust. Tokenisation is simply the next wave it has always known how to take.

Legal certainty. English law governs the rulebook the wholesale world already runs on — the Global Master Repurchase Agreement for repo, and the ISDA Master Agreement for derivatives. And in December 2025, the Property (Digital Assets etc) Act made tokenised assets property under English law: ownable, pledgeable, enforceable. The trade and the asset are certain.

Concentration. Liquidity begets liquidity. The banks, the funds, the corporates, the infrastructure and the talent are already clustered in one venue — which is why London accounts for 37.8% of global foreign exchange and 49.6% of over-the-counter interest-rate derivatives, the largest centre in the world for both. Not because of sterling. Because everyone is already in the room.

Convenience. London sits between the Asian close and the US open — the deepest liquidity window of the global day — and there, the trade settles, funds and documents in one place, one law, one language. It is simply easy to do here. That ease is the flywheel that keeps buyers and sellers coming back.

Beneath all four sits stability: predictable rule of law, an independent central bank, institutions that endure. Together they produce the two things the definition demands — trust and liquidity — and they are exactly what a tokenised market will still need.

A necessary condition, mistaken for a sufficient one

Here is where the current debate goes wrong, thoughtfully and with the best intentions.

The most serious version of the tokenisation argument holds that what makes a market real is the settlement asset — that keeping the cash leg in central bank money is what preserves finality and legal certainty, and separates a serious build from a pilot. Projects like Banque de France and Euroclear's Pythagore, settling tokenised commercial paper in wholesale central bank money, are held up as the proof.

And they are right — as far as they go. Central bank money settlement is a necessary condition for a safe wholesale market. But it is not sufficient to create one. You can settle in the safest money on earth and still have no market, because liquidity, price formation and the rules of participation are properties of the venue, not of what the cash leg is denominated in. Pythagore proves that tokenised paper can be settled cleanly. It does not prove that a market formed. Settlement is the plumbing. The venue is the market.

To argue from the settlement asset is to identify a necessary condition and mistake it for the sufficient one. That is the gap in the debate, and it is where this piece begins.

None of this is a problem in today's data. London's share is stable; its turnover is growing. That is precisely why it is easy to miss.

But the venue for the next market — where assets are issued, funded and settled onchain — is already forming, and it is forming offshore. The live, at-scale tokenised issuance and repo venues are in Switzerland, Hong Kong, Singapore and the United States; the first live onchain US Treasury repo through a major post-trade utility was executed in July 2026, on American infrastructure. The UK's own sovereign digital instrument is still a pilot.

This matters more than it looks, because of one fact the asset-first debate obscures: repo and foreign exchange are the same flow. An FX swap is a repo in two currencies — a spot leg and a reversing forward, each funded at its own repo rate. FX swaps are the single most-traded instrument in the market, at $4 trillion a day. So the wholesale funding layer, the FX book and the repo trial are one continuous, cross-border, always-on flow. When that flow moves onchain, the venue moves with it — by definition, because the venue has only ever been where the flow clears.

So the watch-points write themselves. Whose law will confer finality on the tokenised leg? The currency of tokenised settlement is being fixed right now, at roughly ninety-nine per cent dollar and well under one per cent sterling. The UK's systemic regime arrives in 2027, behind both the EU and the US. And the largest wholesale market Britain leads — foreign exchange — has no issuer, no sponsor, and no workstream that names it, because it is a flow and not an instrument, and policy is built to regulate instruments.

Buyers, sellers, and a place to trade

From where I sit, much of the move to open markets is being done in old ways — behind closed doors, by invitation, and with investment hurdles that lock out innovators. Coordination is hard. But you don't reach open, shared, composable infrastructure by owning the rail, the network and the structural advantage — that is not the right tool for this era. What got us here will not get us there.

None of this is a warning against the builders, assets or technology. The venue is buyers, sellers, and a place to meet — the equation the Champagne fairs solved, that Lloyd's coffee house solved. We already know the patterns. What we need is a new way to make today's workflows visible, and a space to co-design the build for digital markets.

That work is open to anyone: take what markets already do — issue, fund, settle, margin, return — and rebuild it, pattern by pattern, in the open. The rails will keep changing. The equation will not. London, of all places, knows how to build the place where everyone comes to meet. It has all of the principles required for a fair and transparent open market — but we must move principles into action.

The venue is there to be won. But as markets move to 24×7 trading, there is no time to wait.
